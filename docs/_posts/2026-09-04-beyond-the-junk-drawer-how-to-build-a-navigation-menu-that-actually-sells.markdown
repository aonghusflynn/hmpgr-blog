Most B2B websites treat their navigation menu like a junk drawer. 

They cram in every link from "History of the Company" to "Privacy Policy," which kills conversions. When a lead lands on your site, they want a solution.

Your navigation is a guided path. It leads your visitor exactly where they need to go to make a buying decision. 

If your menu is cluttered, you force your leads to think. In web design, if they have to think, they leave.

## The rule of seven

Humans struggle to process more than seven items at once. When your header has ten different tabs, the brain skips over them. 

Audit your menu. Remove anything that doesn't help a prospect understand your value or take the next step. 

### What to keep
*   **The Product/Service:** What do you do?
*   **Solutions/Use Cases:** Who do you do it for?
*   **Pricing:** (Yes, even in B2B). Transparency builds trust.
*   **Resources/Blog:** Show your expertise.

### What to move to the footer
*   Careers.
*   Press Releases.
*   Legal links.
*   Investor relations.

## Use descriptive labels, not jargon

Stop using "Solutions" as a catch-all if you can be more specific. 

If you sell CRM software for architects, call the tab "CRM for Architects." Clarity wins over cleverness every time. Avoid internal project names or "vague-speak" that requires a secret decoder ring to understand.

Your visitors scan your menu in seconds. They are looking for keywords that match their pain points. Give them those keywords.

## The one-button rule

Your primary Call to Action (CTA) is the most visually distinct item in your header. 

Typically, this is the "Get a Demo" or "Start Free Trial" button in the top right corner. Do not give it a neighbor. If you have "Login" and "Sign Up" right next to each other with the same styling, you create friction. 

Make your "Growth" button pop with a high-contrast color. Make the "Login" button a simple text link.

### Pro tip: the mobile-first hierarchy
On mobile, reorder your links. Put your most important conversion page at the top of the list. If a user has to scroll inside your menu to find the "Contact" button, you have lost the lead. Simply hiding the desktop menu behind a "hamburger" icon is not enough.
